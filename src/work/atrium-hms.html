2Connected systems: the product, and the platform

01The problem

Most hotel software is retrofitted from something else — a generic ERP, a restaurant POS, a booking widget — so a property ends up running several systems that each hold part of the truth.

The reservation system does not know what the restaurant sold. The restaurant does not know what the store room has. Nobody has one number for the night.

02Two systems, not one

The structural decision, made at MVP: Atrium is not one product but two connected ones.

  • The public site and CRM. Where a resort finds the product, signs itself up, and becomes a tenant record.
  • The resort management system. What that tenant then runs its front office, inventory and housekeeping on.

A resort registers itself online, is provisioned as its own tenant, and receives a welcome email with its access details — no sales call in the middle. That is what makes it a SaaS product rather than software delivered per property, and it is a design problem as much as an engineering one: the marketing site and the first-run experience are the same journey.

03Built with AI in the loop

Three modules to twelve in eight weeks. That is not a normal enterprise timeline, and it was not reached the normal way — the build ran on an AI-driven development lifecycle.

What that changes is where the hard part sits:

  • Judgement becomes the bottleneck. When screens get built faster than they get decided, choosing is the work — eight housekeeping states, a booking that holds several banquets, which two nights count as the weekend.
  • Consistency has to be deliberate. Twelve modules generated fast drift into twelve dialects. One service form reused across four modules is the thing speed most threatens.
  • Domain knowledge is what AI cannot supply. No model tells you a Bangladeshi wedding is three events.

AI removed the reason to ship a thin product. Not the reason to think about one.

How one night becomes one number

The problem at the top of this page was that the reservation system does not know what the restaurant sold. This is the path that fixes it — one guest, nine steps, every module touched once.

Win the stay

  1. 1

    Quote the whole thing

    Rooms, a hall, a menu and a driver on one document, with a validity date. Quotation

  2. 2

    Hold the space

    A room booking, or a banquet that stays pending until the contract and the advance arrive. Front Office · Banquet

  3. 3

    Take the advance

    A deposit against the booking, receipted by the template that event fires. Front Office · Email

Run the stay

  1. 4

    Check in

    Against the booking, or off the street. A corporate guest bills to account on the credit limit held on their company record. Front Office · Corporate Clients

  2. 5

    Turn the room

    The board moves it through dirty, assigned, in progress, clean — while occupancy tracks separately. Housekeeping

  3. 6

    Everything they use lands on the folio

    Kayaking, dinner, laundry — posted through the same service form, each carrying service charge and VAT. Activities · F&B · Front Office

Close the night

  1. 7

    The kitchen draws on stock

    That dinner was a recipe. The restaurant is a cost centre mapped to a warehouse, so selling it moves real stock. F&B · Inventory

  2. 8

    Every movement hits a ledger code

    Each item carries an asset code and an expense code, so consumption books itself instead of being re-keyed. Inventory

  3. 9

    Settle, and say thank you

    Payment against the check-in, the folio closes, the check-out template sends. One night, one number. Front Office · Email

Three joins hold it together: two ledger codes on every item, commercial terms stored on the company rather than the booking, and everything — departments, restaurants, stores — mapped to a warehouse. That last one is why a dish sold in step 6 decrements a store room in step 7.

04What was held back

Six things were deliberately kept out of v2.0.0, and the release note says so by name rather than leaving people to find the gaps:

  • Data auto-population — linked records carrying values forward. Banquet first, then the rest.
  • Finance & Accounts, and HR.
  • Scheduled and emailed reports — reports run on demand only.
  • External notification channels — notifications stay in-application.
  • Survey and guest feedback — designed, not built.

That last line is the interesting one. The survey module exists as a full design with pre-arrival, post-checkout, banquet and restaurant triggers, and it was still held back. Shipping the design without the build, and saying which is which, is a harder call than quietly dropping it.

Front Office

Front Office is the module the property actually lives in. Eighteen screens sit behind one menu, and a receptionist touches most of them before lunch — so the design problem was never the individual form. It was making eighteen screens feel like one desk.

Three decisions carry it. The dashboard answers the only questions a shift starts with, so nobody has to go looking. Every long task — booking, check-in — is one record split across tabs rather than a wizard, so a half-finished booking is still a booking. And every list opens with its filters already visible, because on a busy desk the search is the screen.

How a stay moves through it

  1. 1

    Book

    Dates, nights, guest, country, billing currency — with the exchange rate locked to it.

  2. 2

    Add the extras

    Airport pickup and drop, then complimentary items — the package's defaults already ticked.

  3. 3

    Take a deposit

    An advance against the booking, in any currency the property accepts.

  4. 4

    Arrive

    Check in against the booking — or straight off the street, with no booking at all.

  5. 5

    Post what they use

    Services onto the folio, with service charge and VAT as switches, not arithmetic.

  6. 6

    Settle

    Payment against the check-in — cash, mobile banking or company transfer.

Housekeeping

A housekeeper does not read a table. They scan a floor, see which rooms are dirty, and go. So Housekeeping is a board, not a list — fifty room cards grouped by room type, each one carrying its own status.

Two things make it work on a busy morning. Every room tracks two independent states at once — how clean it is, and whether anyone is in it — because a room can be dirty and vacant, or clean and occupied, and those need different actions. And the action panel is always on screen rather than appearing after a selection, so its empty state can tell you what to do next: select one or more rooms to change their housekeeping status.

Status is never colour alone. The card carries a coloured edge and the word, so it survives a quick scan and a colour-blind user equally.

Eight cleaning states

  • Clean, Dirty, Assigned to Clean, Cleaning in Progress — the working cycle a room moves through every day.
  • Out of Order, Out of Service, Under Maintenance, House Use — the four ways a room leaves sale without being sold.
  • Alongside them, four occupancy counters — reserved, today's check-in, occupied, expected departure — so the board answers “what can I clean now?” without opening Front Office.

Activities

Activities is what turns a room night into a package — kayaking, fishing, a tree-top course, a driver for the day. It looks like a small module and it is where two quiet decisions show.

The first: an activity is not owned by Activities. Every service carries a parent category naming the module that actually sells it — Front Office for what a resident guest books at the desk, Banquet for what an event package includes. One price list, several front doors.

The second: activities are the only thing here a non-resident can buy. So this Service Entry — the same component used in Front Office and Housekeeping — grows one extra field the others do not have: a guest type that lets the bill go to an in-house room or to a walk-in customer. One shared form, one honest exception.

F&B

F&B is the module with two clocks. A manager wants revenue by payment method and a seven-day trend; a waiter wants to get four items onto a ticket before the table looks up. Those are different products wearing the same navigation, and most hotel software picks one and makes the other suffer.

The split is straight down the middle. The dashboard is read-only and refreshes itself every sixty seconds, with the live counters marked Live so nobody wonders whether the number is stale. The POS is the opposite — a food grid on the left, a running order on the right, totals recalculating as items go in, and two ways out: fire the ticket to the kitchen, or place the order outright.

Underneath both, the same idea repeats: a restaurant is a cost centre mapped to a warehouse, so what the kitchen sells is already the thing Inventory decrements. The plumbing was designed before the pretty part.

What the module holds

  • A dashboard — orders, pending, ongoing kitchen tickets, completed; revenue split six ways by payment method; a seven-day revenue chart; and floors ranked by revenue.
  • Table reservations — with pax and occasion on the row, and a status strip that counts confirmed, seated, unallocated and cancelled before you read a single line.
  • A point of sale — food grid, category chips, customer type, waiter, table, pax, and a live order summary carrying VAT, service charge and supplementary duty.
  • The setup behind it — restaurants as cost centres, card terminals, and a food catalogue six levels wide: items, categories, menu types, item groups, variants, add-ons.

Quotation

A resort rarely sells one thing. A wedding enquiry is rooms and a banquet hall and a menu and a driver, and until the guest sees one number for all of it, they cannot say yes.

Every module in Atrium can price its own line. Quotation is the module that puts them in one document — room, banquet, F&B and services in a single quotation, sectioned A, B, C, D, each section carrying its own subtotal, with the final amount recalculating in a rail that stays on screen while you build.

Two details make it a sales tool rather than a form. It has a validity date, because a held rate that never expires is not a quote. And it ends in two different buttons — save a draft, or save and send to the customer — so the act of quoting and the act of committing to a price are not the same click.

Task Manager

Every property already runs on tasks — the lift needs servicing, the pool needs testing, room 205 needs a new mirror. Task Manager is the module that stops those living in a WhatsApp group.

The design decision that matters most here is small and easy to miss: planned dates and actual dates are separate fields. A task must have a planned start and a due date; it may or may not have an actual start and an actual end. That split is what turns a to-do list into something a manager can report on — without it you can see what is late, but never by how much, or which department is always late.

The rest follows the same instinct: say the thing rather than hide it. The phase field stays visible but disabled, telling you to pick a project first, instead of appearing out of nowhere. The assignee label admits it can be set later, so a task can exist before an owner does. And the due date is labelled planned finish, because the two words mean different things to the person filling the form.

Five statuses, not three

  • To do, In progress, Done — the obvious three, dragged between columns.
  • Blocked and In review get their own columns rather than being folded into in progress. Those are the two places work actually stalls, and a three-column board hides exactly the thing a manager needs to see.
  • The same tasks render as a list or a board, filtered by project, department, assignee, priority, and open-only — one dataset, two habits of working.

One more thing worth saying out loud, because it is a multi-tenant product: every empty state is designed. Nothing in To do. No tasks found — create a task or adjust your filters. No files attached. Every property's first day on Atrium is an empty board, so the empty board is the first screen the product is judged on.

Banquet

Banquet is the module that had to be designed for Bangladesh rather than translated into it.

The decision it turns on: one booking can hold several banquets. A wedding here is not an event, it is a holud, a wedding and a reception — different halls, different days, different headcounts, one client, one contract, one bill. Most event software models a booking as a single date and forces the family to make three unrelated reservations. This one puts a repeating panel inside the booking: fill it, press add, and each banquet joins the list beneath.

The second decision is a status rule, and it is written into the page header rather than buried in a spec: a booking becomes an event order once the contract and the advance are in. Pending until the money and the paper arrive. The team using it never has to ask what confirmed means.

How it holds together

  • The same three tabs as a room booking — details, other services, advance payment. An event is a stay with a hall instead of a bed, so it should not need a new mental model.
  • Priced as you build. Hall rate, adult and child pax, discount, negotiated rate, additional charges and service charge, with a summary rail that starts by telling you what it still needs before it can price anything.
  • A calendar in day, week and month, filterable by hall, with pending events labelled as pending on the entry itself — so a provisional hold never reads as a confirmed one.
  • Occasion as a managed list, not free text, so night stay, banquet, day-long event, wedding and picnic stay countable in reporting.

Corporate Clients

This is the smallest-looking module in Atrium and the one the most other screens depend on. A resort's real money is not the walk-in guest — it is the bank that books forty room nights a quarter and the garments company that runs its annual outing there. Those are accounts, not bookings.

Two fields on the company record explain a pattern that shows up everywhere else in the product.

  • Commercial terms — discount, service charge and VAT, agreed once and stored on the company. That is why every pricing screen in Atrium shows a rack rate and a negotiated rate side by side: the second one is not typed from memory, it comes from here.
  • Credit limit — because a corporate guest does not settle at the desk on the way out. That single number is what makes the corporate guest switch on the Front Office check-in mean anything, and what lets a folio close without a payment.

The rest is quiet structural work: an account manager on every company so someone owns the relationship, a billing address that can differ from the registered one, and business type kept as a managed list — airline, travel agency, government, NGO, IT, garments, pharmaceuticals, telecom, banking, conglomerate — rather than free text, so the sales side can count which sectors actually book.

Inventory

This is the largest module in Atrium and the one that makes the claim at the top of this page true. The problem was never that a resort cannot count its towels. It is that the restaurant, the store room and the accounts office each hold a different version of the same number.

The fix is a field, not a feature. Every inventory item carries two chart-of-accounts codes — one for the asset, one for the expense. A bar of soap is not a bar of soap; it is a balance held at one ledger code that becomes a cost at another the moment housekeeping consumes it. Design that in at the item level and the night's numbers reconcile themselves. Leave it out and someone re-keys the whole month.

The chain, and the way around it

  • Requisition → approval → purchase order → goods received. A department asks, someone approves, a supplier is ordered from, stock arrives against that order with a GRN number and a payment status. Four screens, one thread.
  • And a spot purchase that skips all of it — recorded explicitly as an ad-hoc stock-in without a purchase order. Procurement policy is real; so is a kitchen that needs onions today. The honest design is to give the shortcut its own name and its own audit trail rather than pretend it will not happen.
  • Two asset classes on one form. A radio at the top of the purchase decides whether what you are buying is inventory or a fixed asset. Consumables get expiry dates; assets get depreciation schedules — straight line or declining balance, a life span in months, a percentage.
  • Everything lands somewhere physical. Warehouses, one flagged default; departments mapped to warehouses; and, from the F&B module, restaurants mapped to warehouses too. That is why a dish sold in the restaurant can decrement a store room without anyone typing it twice.

The dashboard sits on top of all of it and answers one question per tile — total value, item count, quantity, and how many items have fallen below their reorder point — then splits activity four ways: purchases, consumption, transfers, adjustments. Those four verbs are the only things that can change a stock number, so they are the only things the chart shows.

Email Management

Every confirmation, welcome and thank-you a guest receives comes out of this module. It is the only part of Atrium the guest actually sees, which makes it the property's voice — and no two properties want to sound alike.

So the model is not one template per event. Each event can hold several templates, and a checkbox decides which one the system sends. A resort can write a short check-out note and a long one, a monsoon-season welcome and an ordinary one, and switch between them without deleting anything or asking support. Six events are wired up — reservation confirmation, check-in welcome, check-out thank you, deposit receipt, quotation, banquet confirmation — the moments a stay actually generates mail.

Two details worth the space

  • Attachments belong to the template, not the send. Anything added here goes out with every email from that template, alongside whatever document the event itself generates — so a property's terms, or a map to the resort, is attached once rather than remembered every time.
  • The sender screen admits a constraint instead of hiding it. Sender addresses are verified through AWS, and the page says plainly that once an address is verified it can no longer be changed. That is an infrastructure limit surfaced in plain language at the moment someone types the address — rather than a support ticket three weeks later.

The editor itself is unremarkable on purpose: a design view and an HTML view, a preview, merge variables in the subject line, a character budget. The person writing a welcome email is a duty manager, not a marketer.

Settings

Settings is where a multi-tenant product either works in a country or quietly does not. Two screens in here carry more design thinking than most of the modules that came before them.

The weekend is not Saturday and Sunday

In Bangladesh the weekend is Friday and Saturday. A hotel system that assumes otherwise prices the wrong two nights of every week, forever, and nobody notices until the year-end numbers are wrong. So which days count as weekend is a setting, not an assumption.

But changing it silently re-prices a month of inventory — which is why the screen does the thing I am most pleased with in this system. Under the checkboxes sits a thirty-day preview: every upcoming day, labelled weekday or weekend, with a dashed border on exactly the cells that would change, and a running count of days affected. It does not ask are you sure. It shows you what will happen, before you commit. Confirmation dialogs make people click yes; consequence made visible makes them think. It even names the timezone the preview starts in, because “today” is not a fixed idea on a cloud product.

Designing against the worst mistake

The IP access page can lock every user out of the property's own system. So before either list, it prints the administrator's own IP address as the server currently sees it, and tells them to whitelist that exact value to keep their access. It also states the precedence rule in one line — the blacklist wins when an address appears on both. The most dangerous screen in the product is the one that explains itself most plainly.

And the rest of what a tenant owns

  • Appearance, company-wide. Five themes plus custom, light / dark / system, brand and accent colour, six typefaces and a base size — and copy that says outright it applies to every user once saved, because one person's taste is about to become everyone's screen.
  • Currency with the base flagged. Ten currencies converted against Bangladeshi Taka, each carrying whether its symbol sits left or right of the number — because a taka sign and a riyal do not sit on the same side.
  • Guest source as a managed list — direct, OTA, travel agent, corporate, referral, walk-in — so the booking channel stays countable rather than typed differently by every receptionist.
  • Policies and terms as rich text, shown to guests and printed onto documents, held in the product rather than in a Word file on somebody's desktop.
  • The workspace slug is locked. It is the tenant's identity in the URL, so the field is visible but disabled — the same honesty as the email module telling you a verified sender can never be changed.
