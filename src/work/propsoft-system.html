The system end to end

Seven features, but one path. A conversation on a site visit becomes a project, a project becomes work, work becomes money owed in both directions, and all of it reports back.

Win the work

  1. 1

    A lead is logged

    Extra work spotted on site gets a projected value and a follow-up date.

  2. 2

    It becomes a project

    Approved work starts on the map: address, client, contact, assigned user.

  3. 3

    The dashboard assigns it

    Filtered, selected, bulk-edited — status moved for a batch at a time.

Do the work

  1. 4

    The field team works it

    Photos come back grouped by work scope, dated, attributed.

  2. 5

    The estimate is approved

    Line by line, with unit cost against actual cost and a status on each.

  3. 6

    A PO goes to the vendor

    Priced, accepted, and reassignable in a batch if the vendor changes.

Settle up

  1. 7

    The client is invoiced

    Invoiced against received, with the receivable that remains and why.

  2. 8

    The vendor is paid

    Status and paid date set for a selection, not one row at a time.

  3. 9

    It all reports back

    Built, saved and compared — which is where the next lead comes from.

Two things sit across the whole path rather than at a point on it. Map & location answers where at any stage, and user & permission decides whose records each role can see at every one of these steps.

For scale: PropSoft.ai reports 5,000+ projects running and 500+ users globally. Those are the product's numbers across four surfaces and a sales team — context for what the system handles, not a result of this work.

Project creation & tracking

A project starts on a map: search an address, drop a pin, attach a client, a contact and an assigned user, then set operational cost and whether follow-up email is automated.

From there it becomes a record with a completion percentage and a status, tracked across seven tabs — photos grouped by work scope, project info, documents, reports, estimate, purchase orders and client invoice. Everything a job accumulates, in the order the work happens.

Creation flow

  1. 1

    Find the property

    Search an address; the map moves to it.

  2. 2

    Drop the pin

    Confirm the exact location. City, state and county fill themselves in.

  3. 3

    Name and address

    Project name, address lines, ZIP.

  4. 4

    Assign the people

    Client, contact person, assigned user — client is the only one required.

  5. 5

    Cost and follow-up

    Field operational cost, a note against it, and whether follow-up email is automated.

  6. 6

    Create

    The project exists: an ID, a status, 0% complete, and seven tabs waiting to fill.

Dynamic dashboard

Two dashboards, one pattern. Projects and purchase orders each open on a dense table with the whole book of work in it — every row a job, every column something someone needs to sort by.

Above the table sit the counts: work grouped by status, by field staff, by client, by completion, by market. A manager arrives needing one slice of several hundred rows, and the row of status chips is how they get there in one click rather than through a filter form.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    Scan the counts

    Status, field staff, client, completion, market — each with a number against it.

  2. 2

    Narrow it down

    One chip for a quick cut; the filter panel when the question is more specific.

  3. 3

    Choose the columns

    Active and inactive columns move between two lists, and reorder.

  4. 4

    Select the rows

    Checkboxes down the left, header checkbox for the page.

  5. 5

    Act on them together

    Bulk edit shared fields, or reassign a batch of POs to another vendor.

  6. 6

    Export

    The filtered view leaves as a file when it needs to go elsewhere.

Lead & revenue generation

Not every job arrives as a work order. Some start as a conversation on a site visit — more work the client has not asked for yet. This is where those become tracked money rather than someone's memory.

The view opens on a date range and three totals: what has been approved, what is still waiting, and what was denied. Underneath, every lead with its projected amount beside its approved amount, so the gap between hoped-for and agreed is visible at a glance.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    Log the lead

    Type, source, organisation and contact — captured while the conversation is fresh.

  2. 2

    Put a number on it

    Projected amount and the date it is expected to land, before anything is agreed.

  3. 3

    Send the summary

    The conversation goes out as an email from the record itself — to, cc, subject, body.

  4. 4

    Set the follow-up

    A date and a note, so the lead resurfaces instead of going quiet.

  5. 5

    Move the status

    Waiting for approval, approved, denied — the state everyone else reads from.

  6. 6

    Read the totals

    Approved, waiting and denied across the chosen date range, at the top of the page.

Map & location

The same book of work as the dashboard, but answering a different question: not what state is this job in, but where is everything.

Every project sits on the map as a pin. Two filters narrow it — state and market — and the zoom level does the rest, with a count in the corner that follows the viewport rather than the filter. Clicking a pin opens the property itself: a photo from site, the address, its status and what it is worth.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    Everything on one map

    Every project as a pin, wherever it is.

  2. 2

    Narrow by state

    Multi-select, so a region is as easy as a single state.

  3. 3

    Narrow by market

    The operational grouping teams actually work to.

  4. 4

    Zoom to refine

    The count in the corner follows the viewport, so the map is itself a filter.

  5. 5

    Open a pin

    A photo from site, the address and the project's status.

  6. 6

    See what it is worth

    The value sits on the card, next to the status.

Dynamic report

Two fixed reports and a builder. Activity covers who did what — check-ins, projects visited, duration, mileage, last image uploaded, and the platform they were on. General covers the shape of the business in charts: projects by state, users by role, what changed today.

The builder is the interesting part. Rather than shipping a report per question, it hands over the parts: pick a report, a type, a date basis and a date range, then add fields and values until the query is the one you meant. Nothing renders until it is asked to — the empty state says so plainly rather than showing a table of nothing.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    Start from the fixed views

    Activity for people, General for the business, both ready without setup.

  2. 2

    Build your own

    Report, type, date basis and range — the four choices that frame every query.

  3. 3

    Add the fields

    Each field brings its own value picker, added and removed one at a time.

  4. 4

    Generate

    Nothing renders until asked. The result regroups by week, quarter, month or year.

  5. 5

    Save it

    A built report becomes a card in My Reports, reusable rather than rebuilt.

  6. 6

    Compare two

    Two saved reports side by side, with a divider you drag to give one more room.

Invoicing & vendor payments

Money moves in two directions, so it gets two dashboards. Vendor payment tracks what goes out — purchase orders, material purchase, payable, paid, final amount. Client payment tracks what comes in — invoiced, received, receivable, and what was written off, discounted or voided along the way.

Both keep the same shape as the rest of the system: a wide sortable table, a filter panel, column control, bulk actions and export. What differs is the stakes. Invoice status is the one column everything downstream reads from, so it carries colour — sent, paid, void, overpaid — where the rest of the table stays plain.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    What goes out

    Purchase orders with material purchase, payable, paid and final amount against each.

  2. 2

    Find the ones in question

    Filter by vendor, status, market, work order or any of the payment dates.

  3. 3

    Mark them paid

    Select rows, set the status and the paid date, save once for the batch.

  4. 4

    What comes in

    Invoiced against received, with the receivable that remains and why it differs.

  5. 5

    Import a batch

    Drag a file in, with a sample to match — payments arrive from the bank, not by hand.

  6. 6

    Read the status

    Sent, paid, void, overpaid — the one column carrying colour, because it is the one that decides what happens next.

User & permission

A maintenance operation is not one kind of user. Field staff, vendors, contact persons, admins, managers, project managers, site engineers, HR, quality assurance, accounting — eleven roles, each seeing a different slice of the same system.

Permissions are set as a grid: every module down the side, four levels of scope across the top — my data, my reportees' data, both, or all of it. That second axis is the part that matters. Most permission screens ask can this role open this page; this one asks whose records can they see when they do.

How it gets used

  1. 1

    See who is in

    Counts per role across the top, then every user as a card with their contact details.

  2. 2

    Invite someone

    Name, contact, which clients they touch, who they report to.

  3. 3

    Pick the role

    Chosen as a card with an illustration, not from a dropdown of job titles.

  4. 4

    Set the matrix

    Modules down, scope across. Parents expand to children so a whole area can be set at once.

  5. 5

    Make a new role

    Name it and inherit an existing role's permissions rather than starting from nothing.

  6. 6

    Lock the built-ins

    Field staff, vendor and contact person cannot be edited — the roles the system itself depends on.
